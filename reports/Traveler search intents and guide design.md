# Answer the screening questions, then reward scrolling

Travelers screen a destination on a short, predictable set of questions before they care about anything else: **when to go (weather), what kind of trip it is, what it costs, and how to get there and how long that takes**. Among travelers comparing destinations, Expedia found weather/seasonality mattered to 53%, trip type to 48%, cost to 47%, getting there to 46% and travel time to 45% ([Hospitality Net / Expedia](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4123087.html)). Safety sits on top of these in US surveys, at 91% "very or extremely important" ([LocaliQ](https://localiq.com/blog/travel-marketing-2026/)). For Philippine islands those generic intents become specific, fast-changing logistics. Since March 2026 there are no direct Manila–El Nido flights. Fees are set by each town and differ (El Nido ₱400, Boracay ₱150 terminal plus ₱300 environmental). Island ATMs run dry, and the right "best months" depend on which side of the archipelago you're on ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/); [Tripwis](https://tripwis.com/things-to-know-before-visiting-boracay-philippines)). Readers scan rather than read: **74% of viewing time falls in the first two screenfuls** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). So the guide should put a verdict and a quick-facts box first and give each later section one small tool that answers a real question: a month-by-month weather strip, a getting-there route card, a budget estimator, an itinerary-length switcher, a cash-and-fees table, and a prep checklist. All of these can be prerendered as plain HTML and hydrated on a static Next.js site. Novelty features such as quizzes, parallax and scrolljacking don't help readers complete tasks and are better left out.

## Weather, trip type, cost and access decide the trip before "things to do" does

No public dataset ranks destination query types ("best time to visit X" vs "things to do in X" vs "is X safe") by search volume, so a ranking has to come from destination-choice surveys plus SEO practice. The surveys are consistent. Most travelers start without a destination: **59% were undecided or weighing several when they first decided to travel**, and only 20% compared similar regions ([Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/path-to-purchase-insights)). That means a guide has to sell the place before it details it. Cost is the biggest barrier to traveling at all (68%) ([LocaliQ](https://localiq.com/blog/travel-marketing-2026/)), and price drives 49% of booking decisions ([Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/path-to-purchase-insights)). SEO practitioners treat "things to do in X" and "best time to visit X" as the core informational head terms ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/travel-seo)). The long tail fills with "is it safe to travel to [country]", entry/visa questions, audience-fit variants ("for families") and price modifiers ([Travelpayouts](https://www.travelpayouts.com/blog/long-tail-travel-keywords-in-the-uk-and-usa/)).

Combining those signals gives the working ranking below. "Things to do" probably has the most raw search volume, but it decides the trip less than weather and cost do. It belongs early in the guide, but not first.

| Rank | Intent | Typical query | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why go / what kind of trip | "is Siargao worth it", "El Nido vs Coron" | Trip type 48%; most travelers are still choosing between places |
| 2 | When to go / weather | "best time to visit Palawan", "typhoon season" | Top choice factor (53%) and top on-trip mishap (26%) |
| 3 | Cost and budget | "how much is a trip to Boracay" | Top barrier (68%), booking driver (49%) |
| 4 | Getting there and travel time | "how to get to El Nido from Manila" | 46% / 45%; heavier for multi-leg island trips |
| 5 | Safety | "is Siargao safe" | 91% call it important; a common question in travel communities |
| 6 | Things to do / tours | "El Nido tour A vs C" | Canonical head term |
| 7 | Where to stay | "where to stay in Coron" | Location complaints drive mishaps |
| 8 | Food | "Siargao restaurants" | Decided mostly on the spot |
| 9 | Getting around | "habal-habal fare", "Grab Siargao" | Comes up during the trip |
| 10 | Practicalities | cash/ATM, SIM, eTravel, packing | Small but trip-breaking if missed |

The rank-4 weighting for island destinations is an inference. Inter-island flights and ferries plausibly make access matter more than the survey average, but no study measures this. Recurring People Also Ask-style questions ("how many days in X", "is X worth it", "X vs Y", "is X expensive") fit naturally as H2s and FAQ entries, though their relative frequency is also unmeasured ([One Mile at a Time](https://onemileatatime.com/insights/travel-destination-safe/)).

### Planning reads deep on desktop; on-trip lookups are fast, mobile and map-driven

A guide does two jobs. Before booking, travelers view **141 pages of travel content over 45 days** and spend about five hours researching (8.7 hours in the US) ([Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/path-to-purchase-insights)). During the trip, the second most common smartphone use on holiday is opening maps for wayfinding (56%) ([VisitAberdeenshire](https://industry.visitabdn.com/news/visitor-insights-highlight-why-mobile-friendly-business-information-matters)). **56% of US travelers decide part of their itinerary "in the moment"** ([Tripadvisor](https://tripadvisor.mediaroom.com/2024-05-14-TRIPADVISOR-PREDICTS-BUSY-SUMMER-TRAVEL-SEASON-FOR-AMERICANS-AN-INCREASING-DESIRE-FOR-ADVENTURE)). The most common on-trip mishaps are bad weather (26%), illness (16%), a dead phone or lost signal (14%) and getting lost (14%) ([Hotel-Online / Aven-Talker](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/travel-mishaps-cost-travelers-nearly-2500)). So the planning read should still work as an on-trip reference: places grouped by area rather than a rigid hourly schedule, rainy-day alternatives, map links, and connectivity advice. Inaccurate information is the main pain point. **51% of travelers who had a mishap blame inaccurate planning information**, at an average cost of $1,891 ([Hotel-Online](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/travel-mishaps-cost-travelers-nearly-2500)). A visible "last checked" date on fast-changing facts is therefore an important feature, not decoration.

## Philippine guides live or die on routes, fees, cash and microclimates

The generic intents above turn into a specific, volatile checklist in the Philippines. Current guides for El Nido, Boracay, Siargao, Bohol and Banaue are organized around the same handful of practical questions, and the facts that age fastest are flight routes, fees and tour prices ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/); [pana.ph](https://pana.ph/community/what-fees-do-i-pay-to-enter-boracay)).

**Getting there changed in 2025–26.** Direct Manila–El Nido flights ended on 29 March 2026. Lio Airport is now reached from Clark (about 1h35, ₱3,500–8,000), Cebu, Caticlan, Bohol and Coron, on turboprops with strict 15 kg checked-bag limits. The overland alternative is a 5–5.5-hour shared van from Puerto Princesa (₱720–790). The Coron–El Nido ferry drops to about two crossings a week from June to September ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/)). Siargao is reached through Clark, Cebu and Davao, though one source says Manila flights ended and Klook still lists them, so this needs checking per guide. The airport is 45–60 minutes from General Luna, and there is no Grab ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/how-to-get-to-siargao/); [Klook](https://www.klook.com/en-PH/blog/siargao-for-beginner-surfers/)). Each guide therefore needs gateway airports with a route-change date, the transfer time and cost to the main town, a sea option with its seasonal frequency, and a backup for weather cancellations. Batanes is notorious for weather-related flight cancellations ([Inquirer](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/918315/pal-express-cancels-batanes-flights-due-to-bad-weather/amp)).

**Fees are local, cash-only and change by ordinance.** El Nido's eco-tourism fee doubled to **₱400 on 1 June 2024**. Lagoon fees (₱200 each) are charged on top of tour prices, and daily caps now apply at Big Lagoon, Small Lagoon and Secret Beach ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/)). Boracay charges a ₱150 Caticlan terminal fee each way plus a ₱150/₱300 environmental fee, and collection is moving to Boracay iPass ([Tripwis](https://tripwis.com/things-to-know-before-visiting-boracay-philippines)). All Boracay fees are under DOT review ([Daily Guardian](https://www.dailyguardian.com.ph/blog/excessive-charges-all-fees-in-boracay-under-review-dot-6-says2sdzxc)). Chocolate Hills is ₱100–150 after a deferred increase ([Bohol Island News](https://boholislandnews.com/2025/09/18/fee-hike-at-chocolate-hills-sparks-concern-gov-aumentado-appeals-for-status-quo/)). Banaue is ₱20 or ₱50 depending on the source ([Wikivoyage](https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Banaue)). Because sources conflict this much, the right format is a small fee table with a "verified [month year]" stamp, not prose.

**Weather is regional, not national.** PAGASA's rule of thumb is a dry season from December to May and a rainy season from June to November ([PAGASA](https://bagong.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/information/climate-philippines)). Under the Coronas climate types, though, western Palawan (Type I) is dry from November to April, while Siargao's Surigao coast (Type II) has **no dry season and its heaviest rain from December to February**. Boracay (Type III) has only a short dry season ([Wikipedia: Climate of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_the_Philippines)). About 20 typhoons a year arrive, mostly between June and November ([UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/philippines)). In El Nido's wet months, 1 in 5 to 1 in 3 tour days are cancelled ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/)). A single national "dry season" line is wrong for several flagship destinations, so each guide needs its own month strip.

**Cash, signal and power round out the essentials.** Siargao's General Luna ATM often accepts only Philippine cards and runs dry on weekends, and visitors are advised to bring ₱5,000–10,000 for five days ([PhilippineBeaches](https://philippinebeaches.org/?p=10409)). Coron's ATMs run out "reliably on weekends and during fiesta". A four-day mid-range El Nido trip needs ₱12,000–15,000 in cash ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/palawan-trip-cost/)). Tourist SIMs and eSIMs must be registered with a passport and are valid for 30 days ([ExpatDen](https://www.expatden.com/philippines/sim-registration-philippines-foreigners/)). There is no signal on the water around El Nido, and a May 2026 grid failure cut power along 240 km of Palawan for about 13 hours ([Suroy.ph](https://suroy.ph/el-nido-travel-guide/)).

**Entry and safety belong in a short country-wide block that every guide links to, not in each guide's body.** Most Western visitors get 30 days visa-free with a passport valid six months beyond the stay and an onward ticket. A domestic flight doesn't count as onward ([Mad Monkey](https://madmonkeyhostels.com/blogs/philippines-visa-guide-tourist-entry-requirements/)). eTravel is free and only legitimate at etravel.gov.ph. Scam sites charge up to about US$100 ([bucket-list-ideas](https://bucket-list-ideas.pages.dev/blog/philippines-etravel-2026-arrival-card-americans/)). The registration window (within 72 hours vs. at least three days before arrival) is unconfirmed on official pages ([Wego](https://blog.wego.com/philippines-etravel-registration/)). The UK advises against travel to much of Mindanao but exempts Siargao, Camiguin and Dinagat ([UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/philippines)), a nuance any Siargao guide's safety line should state. Common scams are airport taxis (one tourist was allegedly charged ₱5,500 between NAIA terminals in August 2026), short-changing and sleight-of-hand money changers ([AutoIndustriya](https://www.autoindustriya.com/auto-industry-news/thai-tourists-alleged-php-55k-naia-taxi-fare-prompts-ltfrb-sco.html); [World Nomads](https://www.worldnomads.com/travel-safety/southeast-asia/philippines/what-are-the-common-scams-in-philippines)). The notes contain no sourced findings on etiquette, local laws, or health risks such as dengue and water safety. Those topics need a separate pass before publication.

## Readers scan headings and hunt for numbers, so structure is the product

NN/g's eye-tracking shows that unformatted text pushes readers into the inefficient F-pattern. Clear, front-loaded headings enable the **layer-cake pattern, "the most effective way to scan"**, in which readers jump from heading to heading ([NN/g layer-cake](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). Readers also use "spotted" scanning, hunting for one number or word, and reading every word is uncommon ([NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Together with attention falling off after the first two or three screens (about 81% of viewing time by screen three), this supports a fixed pattern. Put a verdict and quick facts in the first screen. Write keyword-first H2s that make sense on their own. Open each section with a one- or two-sentence answer, then a table, cards or a short list. A sticky table of contents helps on long pages, because a static one at the top scrolls out of view quickly ([NN/g in-page links](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/in-page-links-content-navigation/)). The cautionary example is Lonely Planet's redesign, which Nomadic Matt criticized for "a lot of space, not a lot of curated information" and for pushing essentials below the fold ([Nomadic Matt](https://www.nomadicmatt.com/travel-blogs/lonely-planet/)).

Accordions cut scrolling but hide content and add interaction cost. They suit independent items readers don't compare ([NN/g accordions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-on-desktop/)), and they hurt when readers need most of the content ([McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/web-services/node/5545)). That makes them right for the FAQ and wrong for "When to go" or "Getting there", which readers cross-reference. No source gives an ideal word count for a destination guide. The evidence points to a principle instead: the content that answers the screening questions goes in the first two or three screens, and everything after that should be easy to skim and easy to skip.

### Interactivity earns its place only when it answers a question

Independent evidence on travel widgets is thin. Most "interactive content doubles dwell time" figures come from vendors such as Outgrow, which claims 4.5 minutes on quizzes vs 1.3 on static pages ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/user-engagement-benchmarks/)), and Riddle, which reports 112 seconds of active time per start ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/riddle-finds-78-of-interactive-content-shares-escape-web-analytics/)). These numbers should be treated as marketing. The stronger case is task support. Wanderlog, a well-regarded trip planner, is built around **an itinerary and a map in one view, with saving and offline access** ([Wanderlog](https://wanderlog.com)). Each tool on the page should answer a top-ranked intent directly: a weather strip answers "when", a budget estimator answers "how much", itinerary tabs answer "how many days", and a route card answers "how do I get there". A checklist and a save-to-trip button give readers something to keep. Quizzes ("which island fits you?") serve the dreaming stage and can bury the useful content, so they work better as a separate page that links into guides.

The scroll itself should stay native. In NN/g testing, a scrolljacked page **disoriented every participant who visited it**, and task-focused users had the least tolerance ([NN/g scrolljacking](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). Parallax is "just not that impressive to users" and can cause motion sickness ([NN/g parallax](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/)). The Pudding advises against steppers and swipe interfaces that override scrolling ([The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)). The recommended approach is light progressive reveal using CSS `animation-timeline` inside `@supports`, animating only `transform` and `opacity`, with content visible by default and under `prefers-reduced-motion` ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/ui-behaviors/scrollytelling)). The site's existing `.reveal` class should be audited against this: if it starts elements at `opacity: 0` without an `@supports` gate, content could be invisible in browsers that don't support scroll-driven animation. Any sticky bar must not hide focused elements (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.11) ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html)). Heavy embeds such as maps should load behind a facade to protect the Core Web Vitals targets (LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS ≤ 0.1) ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)).

## Recommended section order and scroll rewards

The order follows the intent ranking, adjusted for Philippine specifics. It sells the destination first, then answers the four screening questions in the first screens, then switches to on-trip reference. Every interactive element below is rendered at build time as semantic HTML (tables, lists, links) from validated frontmatter or JSON, so the page is complete without JavaScript, and a small client component adds interactivity on top. The existing `toc` from `##` headings, `components/FAQ.tsx`, and FAQPage JSON-LD carry over directly.

| # | Section (H2, keyword-first) | Answers | Interactive element along the scroll | Static-site implementation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Hero + one-line verdict | Why go, who it's for | "Good for" tag chips (beaches, surf, diving) linking to `/articles?category=` | Plain links; `fetchpriority="high"` hero image |
| 1 | At a glance | Spotted scanning for numbers | Quick-facts card: best months, gateway + travel time, days needed, ₱/day range, cash needed, signal; "Last checked" stamp; Save-to-trip button | Frontmatter fields validated in `articles.ts`; button with `aria-pressed`, `localStorage` in try/catch |
| 2 | Sticky jump bar | Navigation | Scroll-spy chips (mobile horizontal row, desktop side rail) | IntersectionObserver, `aria-current`, `scroll-margin-top` on headings |
| 3 | Why go (3–5 bullets) + vs. nearby | Trip type, comparison | Compact "X vs Y" two-column card linking to the sibling guide | Static markup |
| 4 | When to go | Weather, typhoons | **12-month strip** colored Dry / Shoulder / Wet-typhoon with text labels; tap a month for a one-line note (surf season, festivals, tour-cancellation risk) | `<table>` with caption + CSS cells; per-destination climate type, not national |
| 5 | Getting there | Route, time, cost | **Route card / stepper-free timeline**: origin tabs (Manila / Clark / Cebu) showing legs, durations, fares, ferry seasonality, weather backup; route-change date | ARIA tabs over prerendered lists; text fallback |
| 6 | Fees and cash | Hidden costs | **Fee table** with amount, where to pay, "verified" date; nearest reliable ATM; "bring ₱X cash" line | Static table; flags the conflicting figures honestly |
| 7 | Budget | Cost | **Budget estimator**: style (budget/mid/luxury) × days × travelers → ₱ and USD range, showing its assumptions | Native `<select>`/range + `<output aria-live="polite">`; static table without JS |
| 8 | Things to do | Activities, tours | Card grid with filter chips (beach, tour, food, rainy-day); tour comparison table (e.g. El Nido A–D) | Cards prerendered; client-side filter only toggles visibility |
| 9 | Itinerary | How many days | **Trip-length tabs** (2 / 3 / 5 days), grouped by area with rainy-day swaps | ARIA tabs; all panels in HTML |
| 10 | Where to stay | Location honesty | **Map facade** (static image → "Load map") with area pins; area cards noting distance, generator/backup power | Fixed aspect-ratio to avoid CLS; text list of the same places |
| 11 | Getting around | Last mile | Fare chips (tricycle, habal-habal, van) with "agree price first" and helmet notes | Static |
| 12 | Before you go | Practicalities | **Prep checklist** (eTravel at the official site only, cash, SIM registration, dry bag, offline maps, buffer day before the international flight) with progress count | Checkboxes saved in `localStorage`; links to a shared country-wide entry/safety page |
| 13 | FAQ | People Also Ask | Accordion: worth it? how many days? safe? best month? ATMs? signal? | Native `<details>`; existing FAQ component + FAQPage JSON-LD |
| 14 | Related guides + newsletter | Next step | Prev/next and "pairs well with" cards | Already automatic |

Two constraints keep the page concise. First, each section opens with a one- or two-sentence direct answer and has a hard cap of about one screen of supporting content. Longer material moves to the FAQ or a linked article. Second, a widget appears only where a top-ten intent calls for one, which spaces the rewards roughly every one to two screens without stacking them. Decorative effects are limited to the existing scroll-driven fade-in and, optionally, a subtle CSS-only reading-progress bar. No evidence shows that progress bars raise completion, so it's cheap to include and fine to drop.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the redesign. A "one-stop" Philippine guide is less about covering everything than about staying current on the six facts that go stale and cause mishaps: route, fee, cash, signal, season and safety zone. Those facts should be structured data with "last checked" dates, validated at build time the way the site already validates slugs and images. Doing that turns them into both the quick-facts card and the interactive widgets, so structure, freshness and interactivity come from the same source. Interactivity here works best as a by-product of well-modeled data rather than an add-on.

The evidence has gaps. There is no measured search-volume ranking for Philippine destination queries, no independent study of travel-widget engagement, and no sourced etiquette or health guidance. Several fees and routes conflict across sources. Two next steps follow: pull Ahrefs or Semrush question data for a few flagship destinations to confirm the order of the H2s and FAQ, and add simple event counts to each widget so the site measures which rewards readers actually use instead of relying on vendor benchmarks. Because Phil Strats is a mock brand, the fee and route figures here work as realistic placeholders. Before any guide is presented as current advice, they would need re-verifying against official LGU, PAGASA and immigration pages.
