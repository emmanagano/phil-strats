# Rizal's Novels Still Anchor Filipino Literary Fame

The most famous books by Filipino authors are José Rizal's *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) and *El Filibusterismo* (1891). A 1956 law requires every Filipino student to read them, they are published in Penguin Classics, and they have been adapted again and again, most recently as GMA's 2022–23 series *Maria Clara at Ibarra*. Around them sits a clear second tier. Inside the Philippines it includes Francisco Balagtas's verse romance *Florante at Laura*. Abroad it includes Carlos Bulosan's *America Is in the Heart* (1946). Then come the martial-law-era Tagalog novels of Lualhati Bautista, Edgardo M. Reyes and Amado V. Hernandez, which reached mass audiences through film, and F. Sionil José's English-language Rosales Saga, which made him the country's perennial Nobel candidate. Popular fame works differently. Bob Ong's comic memoir *ABNKKBSNPLAko?!* sold about 240,000 copies, and Wattpad romances like *She's Dating the Gangster* became box-office films, in a market where a literary novel struggles to sell 1,000 copies a year. In the diaspora, Jessica Hagedorn's *Dogeaters* is the landmark novel. The biggest commercial hits are by young-adult and children's authors such as Melissa de la Cruz and the two-time Newbery winner Erin Entrada Kelly. Patricia Evangelista's *Some People Need Killing* (2023) is the most-honored recent nonfiction. Three different things make these books famous: law and the school curriculum, film adaptation, and recognition from US and UK prizes and publishers. Each era's canon reflects which of those forces was strongest at the time.

## A 1956 law made two Rizal novels compulsory reading

Rizal's two novels are the only Filipino books that nearly every Filipino has studied. *Noli Me Tángere* follows Crisóstomo Ibarra, who comes home to open a reformist school, is framed for a failed uprising, and loses María Clara to a family secret. It is a satire of friar abuse under Spanish rule. Rizal had it **printed in Berlin in 1887, 2,000 copies paid for with a ₱300 loan from Máximo Viola** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noli_Me_T%C3%A1ngere_(novel))). The darker sequel, *El Filibusterismo*, brings Ibarra back in disguise to plot revolution. Spanish authorities banned both books. Their place today rests on the **Rizal Law (Republic Act No. 1425), signed June 12, 1956**. It requires courses on Rizal's life and works, "especially" these two novels, in every public and private school, college and university. The Catholic Church fought the law because the novels attack the clergy ([ChanRobles: RA 1425](https://chanrobles.com/republicactno1425.html); [Wikipedia: Rizal Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rizal_Law)). Under K-12, students read the *Noli* in Grade 9 and the *Fili* in Grade 10 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noli_Me_T%C3%A1ngere_(novel))).

The novels are also famous abroad. The *Noli* has at least six English translations, from *Friars and Filipinos* (1911) to Harold Augenbraum's **Penguin Classics edition (2006)**, and has been translated into French, Dutch, German, Indonesian, Korean and Czech, among others ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noli_Me_T%C3%A1ngere_(novel)); [Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/291131/noli-me-tangere-touch-me-not-by-jose-rizal)). Adaptations span more than a century. They include silent films in 1915 and 1930; a 1957 Tagalog opera; Gerardo de León's 1961 film, which won five FAMAS Awards including Best Picture; a 1995 musical with music by Ryan Cayabyab; and the 2019 musical film *Damaso* ([Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Noli_Me_T%C3%A1ngere_(1961_film))). The most recent, **GMA's *Maria Clara at Ibarra* (October 2022 to February 2023)**, sends a modern student into the world of the novels, which shows the books are still mainstream entertainment and not only homework ([GMA Network](https://www.gmanetwork.com/entertainment/showbiznews/maria-clara-at-ibarra-marks-milestone-achievements-in-its-first-two-weeks/93960)).

Inside the Philippines, Balagtas's ***Florante at Laura*** is the third essential book. It is a Tagalog *awit* (verse romance) set in Albania, in which the betrayed nobleman Florante is rescued by the Persian Muslim Aladin. It is traditionally dated 1838, although the earliest surviving edition is from 1853. Wikipedia calls it the only Philippine poem that has stayed in print continuously since it first appeared ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florante_at_Laura)). Grade 8 students spend weeks on it under DepEd's MATATAG curriculum ([DepEd LRMDS](https://lrmds.deped.gov.ph/detail/6997)). A ballet version premiered in 2024. Unlike Rizal, though, *Florante* has no major international translation, so it is well known at home and little known abroad.

## Penguin Classics built the English-language canon

The second classic tier is the generation that wrote in English under American rule. Penguin Classics has now published several of these writers, which is the clearest sign of international canon status. **Carlos Bulosan's *America Is in the Heart* (Harcourt, Brace, 1946)** is the best-known pre-1960 Filipino book abroad after Rizal. It is a semi-autobiographical account of a boy from rural Pangasinan who becomes a migrant laborer facing racism in the American West ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/carlos-bulosan)). The book became a symbol of the 1970s Filipino American identity movement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_Is_in_the_Heart)). Penguin Classics reissued it in 2019 with a foreword by Elaine Castillo ([Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/600643/america-is-in-the-heart-by-carlos-bulosan-foreword-by-elaine-castillo-introduction-by-e-san-juan-jr-selected-by-jeffrey-arellano-cabusao/)).

**Nick Joaquin**, named National Artist in 1976, is the central figure of Philippine writing in English. His play *A Portrait of the Artist as Filipino* (written 1950) and stories such as "May Day Eve" are school staples. His novel *The Woman Who Had Two Navels* (1961) won the Stonehill Award. For his centenary in 2017, Penguin Classics published *The Woman Who Had Two Navels and Tales of the Tropical Gothic*, making him **the third Filipino in Penguin Classics, after Rizal and José Garcia Villa** ([ABS-CBN](https://abs-cbn.com/life/04/28/17/penguin-classics-honors-nick-joaquin-with-new-collection); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2017/04/19/523586256/a-potent-uneasy-blend-of-passion-and-fatalism-in-the-woman-who-had-two-navels)). Villa, who lived in New York from 1930, is famous more as a poet figure ("Doveglion", whom E. E. Cummings addressed in a poem) than for any single book. His *Have Come, Am Here* (Viking, 1942) introduced his "reversed consonance" rhyme, and his comma poems followed in *Volume Two* (1949). Penguin collected his work as *Doveglion: Collected Poems* (2008) ([Poets.org](https://poets.org/poet/jose-garcia-villa); [Inquirer USA](https://usa.inquirer.net/16254/jose-garcia-villa-honored-in-ny-as-penguin-publishes-his-poems)). Some sources say he was a Pulitzer finalist or won the Bollingen Prize, but neither claim is well confirmed.

The other famous English-language classics are short stories, not books. Paz Márquez-Benítez's **"Dead Stars" (1925)** is widely regarded as the first modern Filipino short story in English ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paz_M%C3%A1rquez-Ben%C3%ADtez)). Manuel Arguilla's **"How My Brother Leon Brought Home a Wife"** is the title story of a 1940 collection that won the first Commonwealth Literary Contest. Arguilla was executed by the Japanese in 1944, and the La Union house that inspired the story still stands ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Arguilla); [Inquirer](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/902364/house-in-literary-classic-defies-time/amp)). Several other books are famous mainly for being firsts, and few people read them today. Pedro Paterno's *Nínay* (1885) is often called the first novel by a Filipino, though critics judge it artistically weak ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%ADnay)). Zoilo Galang's *A Child of Sorrow* (1921) is considered the first Philippine novel in English ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Child_of_Sorrow)). Stevan Javellana's *Without Seeing the Dawn* (1947) is called the first post-war Filipino novel in English, and Lino Brocka filmed it as *Santiago!* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Without_Seeing_the_Dawn)). Lope K. Santos's *Banaag at Sikat* (1906) is called "the fountainhead of social realism in the Tagalog novel" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banaag_at_Sikat)).

## Film turned martial-law novels into household names

After 1960, the books ordinary Filipinos know best are Tagalog novels about injustice and dictatorship, and film made most of them famous. **Lualhati Bautista** won Palanca grand prizes for three novels: *Gapô* (1980), ***Dekada '70*** (1983) and ***Bata, Bata… Pa'no Ka Ginawa?*** (1984) ([Philstar](https://www.philstar.com/headlines/2023/02/12/2244433/writer-activist-lualhati-bautista-dies-77)). *Dekada '70* follows a middle-class family through Marcos-era martial law. Chito S. Roño's 2002 film, starring Vilma Santos, won Best Picture at the Metro Manila Film Festival, and Santos also starred in the film of *Bata, Bata* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dekada_%2770_(film)); [Inquirer](https://entertainment.inquirer.net/485672/vilma-santos-carlo-aquino-react-to-lualhati-bautistas-death)). In 2022, a Penguin Classics editor approached her about publishing *Dekada '70* as a Penguin Classic, a year before her death in February 2023 ([Rappler](https://www.rappler.com/life-and-style/literature/lualhati-bautista-gets-offer-penguin-publish-dekada-70/)). **Edgardo M. Reyes's *Sa mga Kuko ng Liwanag*** (serialized 1966–67) follows a fisherman, Julio, through Manila's underbelly as he searches for his lost love, Ligaya. Its fame comes largely from Lino Brocka's film adaptation, *Maynila: Sa mga Kuko ng Liwanag* ([Wikipedia](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Claws_of_Brightness)). **Amado V. Hernandez**, a National Artist from the first batch in 1973, wrote much of ***Mga Ibong Mandaragit*** (1969) while jailed from 1951 to 1956. It is a socio-political novel about justice during and after the Japanese occupation, and Penguin Southeast Asia has published it in English as *The Birds of Prey* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amado_V._Hernandez); [Penguin SG](https://www.penguin.sg/book_author/amado-v-hernandez/)).

In English, the leading modern figure is **F. Sionil José** (1924–2022). His five-novel **Rosales Saga** (*The Pretenders*, *Tree*, *My Brother, My Executioner*, *Mass* and *Po-on*) traces about a century of Philippine history (1872–1972) through his hometown in Pangasinan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Sionil_Jos%C3%A9)). He won the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1980 and was named National Artist in 2001, and he was often described as the leading Filipino candidate for the Nobel Prize ([RMAF](https://rmaward.asia/tribute-for-francisco-sionil-jose/); [CCP](https://culturalcenter.gov.ph/press-release/ccp-mourns-national-artist-f-sionil-jose/)). These writers are famous in different ways. José and Joaquin carry prestige and reach readers abroad. Bautista and Reyes are probably more widely known at home, yet **neither was ever named National Artist**. The award has gone to many writers honored chiefly as poets, critics and scholars, such as Virgilio Almario, Bienvenido Lumbera and Resil Mojares, while some of the most widely read novelists were left out ([Wikipedia: National Artist list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Artist_of_the_Philippines)).

## Bob Ong and Wattpad outsell the literary canon

Measured by copies sold, Philippine-based fame looks completely different. The pseudonymous **Bob Ong's *ABNKKBSNPLAko?!* (Visprint, 2001)** is a comic memoir of Filipino schooling. Its first print run of 500 copies sold out, and **about 240,000 copies had sold by 2012–13**, making it Visprint's top title. Viva Films adapted it in 2014, and Tuttle published it in English as *The Boy with a Snake in His Schoolbag* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABNKKBSNPLAko%3F!); [WHSmith](https://www.whsmith.co.uk/products/the-boy-with-a-snake-in-his-schoolbag-a-memoir-from-manila-or-something-like-that/bob-ong/freely-abrigo/hardback/9780804855211.html)). **Ricky Lee**, the country's most-awarded screenwriter, sells novels like popular fiction. *Para kay B* (2008) sold about 30,000 copies in two years, and *Si Amapola sa 65 na Kabanata* sold about 10,000 within days. A 2012 Philstar report noted that literary authors "find it difficult to dispose of the first print order of 1,000 copies in a year" ([Philstar](https://philstar.com/entertainment/2012/01/27/771382/building-audience-books-movies)). Lee was named National Artist in 2022 for Film and Broadcast Arts, not Literature ([NCCA](https://ncca.gov.ph/?p=26371)).

The largest engine of popular fiction is **Wattpad**. The Philippines is the platform's second-largest market, and Wattpad says the country has adapted more of its stories into print, TV and film than any other ([BusinessWorld](https://www.bworldonline.com/?p=154593)). Bianca Bernardino's ***She's Dating the Gangster*** topped National Book Store's bestseller list and sold more than 50,000 copies in print before its 2014 Star Cinema film. ***Diary ng Panget*** by HaveYouSeenThisGirL drew more than 12 million reads, and its first three books sold 140,000 copies combined ([Vera Files](https://verafiles.org/articles/book-power-perks-up-the-movies)). The pipeline is still running: *He's Into Her* became a 2021 series, and Viva has *Hell University* and other adaptations planned for 2026 ([Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/02/06/the-complicated-comeback-of-wattpad-adaptations)). Recent literary prizes, by contrast, have gone to university-press novels such as Glenn Diaz's *Yñiga* and Vin dela Serna Lopez's *1762*, both National Book Award winners for Best Novel in English, but there is no evidence of mass sales for these ([Inquirer](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1873152/nbdb-national-book-awards-winners); [PhilstarLife](https://philstarlife.com/news-and-views/711614-42nd-national-book-awards-winners)).

## US prizes and bestseller lists define diaspora fame

For writers abroad, and for Philippine-based writers published by international houses, fame is measured by US and UK prizes and bestseller lists. **Jessica Hagedorn's *Dogeaters* (1990)**, a many-voiced novel of Marcos-era Manila, was a **National Book Award finalist** and won the American Book Award. Penguin reissued it in 2024 ([Poetry Foundation](https://poetryfoundation.org/poets/jessica-hagedorn); [Readings](https://readings.com.au/product/9780143138167/dogeaters--jessica-hagedorn--2024--9780143138167)). Bienvenido N. Santos's *Scent of Apples* (1979), stories of lonely Filipino "oldtimers" in America, won the inaugural American Book Award ([SFADB](https://www.sfadb.com/The_American_Book_Awards_1980)). Ninotchka Rosca's *State of War* (1988) is often listed with these books, but the American Book Award commonly credited to her *Twice Blessed* could not be verified.

The most-decorated recent book is **Patricia Evangelista's *Some People Need Killing* (2023)**. Evangelista is a Philippines-based journalist, and the book is her account of Duterte's drug war. It was named one of the **New York Times' 10 Best Books of 2023**, was longlisted for the Women's Prize for Non-Fiction, and won the New York Public Library's Helen Bernstein Award ([Rappler](https://www.rappler.com/?p=2666698); [Library Journal](https://libraryjournal.com/story/patricia-evangelista-wins-nypl-bernstein-award-for-excellence-in-journalism-book-pulse)). In literary fiction, **Miguel Syjuco's *Ilustrado*** won the 2008 Man Asian Literary Prize while still a manuscript and was a New York Times Notable Book of 2010 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Syjuco)). Mia Alvar's *In the Country* won the 2016 PEN/Robert W. Bingham Prize ([PEN America](https://pen.org/2016-penrobert-w-bingham-prize/)). Gina Apostol's *Insurrecto*, about the Balangiga massacre, was one of Publishers Weekly's 10 best books of 2018, and her *Gun Dealers' Daughter* won the PEN/Open Book Award ([PEN America](https://pen.org/?p=37465)). Elaine Castillo's *America Is Not the Heart* (2018), whose title answers Bulosan, appeared on many best-of-year lists ([PRH](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/authors/2144051/elaine-castillo)).

The biggest sellers, however, write young-adult, children's and genre fiction. Manila-born **Melissa de la Cruz**'s *Isle of the Lost* spent **more than 50 weeks on the New York Times list, 15 of them at No. 1, with over a million copies in print** ([PRH](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/728965/isle-of-the-lost-the-a-descendants-novel-vol-1-by-melissa-de-la-cruz/9781484712955)). **Erin Entrada Kelly**'s *Hello, Universe*, which draws on Filipino folklore, won the **2018 Newbery Medal**, and Netflix announced a film adaptation ([Netflix](https://about.netflix.com/en/news/netflix-to-produce-hello-universe-movie-based-on-newbery-award-winning-novel-from-erin-entrada-kelly)); she later won a second Newbery for *The First State of Being* ([B&N](https://m.barnesandnoble.com/w/the-first-state-of-being/erin-entrada-kelly/1143593914)). Bacolod-based **Thea Guanzon**'s fantasy *The Hurricane Wars* (2023) reached about No. 12 on the New York Times list ([Inquirer USA](https://usa.inquirer.net/138446/this-filipina-authors-debut-novel-made-it-to-the-new-york-times-bestsellers-list)); one local headline called her the first Filipino New York Times bestseller, which is wrong given de la Cruz and Kelly. Randy Ribay's *Patron Saints of Nothing* was a 2019 National Book Award finalist ([National Book Foundation](https://www.nationalbook.org/?p=24808)), Mia P. Manansala's *Arsenic and Adobo* won the Agatha Award for Best First Novel ([PRH Global](https://global.penguinrandomhouse.com/announcements/berkley-author-mia-p-manansala-wins-agatha-award-for-best-first-novel/)), and Aimee Nezhukumatathil, whose mother is Filipina, reached No. 5 on the New York Times list with the essay collection *World of Wonders* in 2020 ([Univ. of Mississippi](https://news.olemiss.edu/?p=102713)).

## Conclusion

Filipino literary fame is split three ways, and the splits rarely overlap. The state decides the domestic canon through the Rizal Law, the curriculum and the National Artist awards. Film and Wattpad decide what ordinary Filipinos actually know and buy. US publishers and prize juries decide what the rest of the world sees. Few books succeed in all three. Rizal's two novels do, and *Dekada '70* may join them if a Penguin Classics edition appears. That would make it the first Tagalog-language novel of the martial-law era to enter the international canon that Rizal, Villa, Joaquin and Bulosan already occupy. The newest pattern is that Philippine-based writers no longer need to emigrate to reach global readers: Evangelista and Guanzon both reached New York Times lists while living in the Philippines.

Several gaps remain. The notes include almost no sales data for literary classics. Several dates conflict between sources, including those for *Florante at Laura*, Joaquin's works and Villa's honors. The notes also do not cover significant figures such as NVM Gonzalez, Mars Ravelo's komiks, Tess Uriza Holthe and the *Trese* comics. For a curated list, the safest picks combine a legal or curricular anchor, a Penguin edition, or a major film or prize. Every title in the table above meets at least one of those tests.
